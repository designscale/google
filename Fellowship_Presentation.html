Slide 1: Open 0:45

Twenty years of teaching, and I have always weighted the iterative process and the holistic approach over the final artifact. That part is not new.

What is new is that once AI can produce good enough work in seconds, a student who relies on it alone cannot prove how they got there. The work looks finished. The thinking is invisible, and none of my rubrics could tell me whether it happened at all.

So I started building a way to grade the thinking directly, instead of reading it off the work. Cognitive-Process Assessment. CPA.

Slide: one AI-generated UI screen, full bleed. One line of type. "This took eleven seconds. What does it prove?"

Delivery. Two sentences carry this slide and both are easy to throw away. "Twenty years of teaching" is the credential, so let it sit for a beat. It is what stops the room hearing CPA as a panic response to AI. "A student who relies on it alone cannot prove how they got there" is the stake, and note whose stake it is. Not a grading inconvenience, a student unable to demonstrate authorship of their own thinking. Every faculty member in the room has a student like that. Say it as their problem, not mine, and the surveillance objection gets much harder to make later.

Slide 2: Grounded at FIT 0:40

Before I built anything, I wanted to know whether the premise held up at my own institution. James Pearce, our Technical Manager for Faculty Research Space and Emerging Technology, confirmed that FIT has no AI detection tools in place and no plan to build any.

If you can't verify AI use from the outside, the only honest path left is to grade the process itself. That is the premise CPA is built on. Not a philosophy I read my way to, a constraint I teach inside of.

Slide: the Pearce quote, attributed, nothing else.

Slide 3: The Workflow 0:47

Research starts human led. Gemini Notebook synthesizes it. Gemini and Gemini Gems direct the UX writing and strategy, with the student acting as copywriter and strategist. Students craft three art directions in Figma and choose one, and only then does Stitch generate the first UI, from a direction the student already owns. Then create visual image and photo assets with Nano Banana. Finalize the design and the craft in Figma.

Domain-specific knowledge is applied at every step, and that is what makes each decision. Students work with AI from the first day. A human makes the decision at every handoff.

Delivery: this is the densest slide. Tool names live on the diagram. If I am pointing and reading, I have lost twenty seconds I needed elsewhere.

Slide 4: What CPA Grades 1:05

Eight dimensions, and the one to name first is error catching. Did the student catch an Attribution Error, a claim the AI simply made up? Or a Logical Error, a step that doesn't follow even from true premises? That language is Google's own, from the REVEAL paper, mapped into the rubric after Session 1.

I have since added a third, and I found it two ways: in my own R&D, and in an empathy interview with one of my students. A Representational Error: when the model returns a demographically skewed default, over and over, against an explicit instruction to do otherwise. Not a fabricated fact, not bad reasoning. A different failure that needs its own name.

And in every class we discuss the what and the why of their work. That is critique. Design education has run on it for a century, so I am not adding a ceremony. I am grading the conversation we were already having.

And it cannot be only written. A written log can be generated in seconds. A live explanation cannot, because I can stop a student and ask why they rejected the other three directions, and we both find out in real time whether they know. That is also where the thinking happens, not just where I check it. Say something out loud to someone who asks questions and you hear your own weak reasoning before they do.

That conversation is what timestamps the record.

Slide: three error cards across the top, Attribution / Logical / Representational, the third marked NEW. Eight dimension chips underneath, Critical Curation highlighted.

Delivery: the critique line is what makes CPA adoptable, so do not throw it away as a closing aside. Every design faculty member in that room runs critique already. I am telling them the verification layer is a ritual they have and a rubric they don't, not a new thing on their calendar.

Slide 5: Think Gemini video 1, full

▶ Roll it cold. No setup, no narration. Say nothing over it. Let it finish, and let the room sit with it for a beat before speaking.

Staging: the room needs to be impressed by the artifact before being told the artifact isn't the point. That is the Slide 1 premise, staged live.

Slide 6: The Judgment Underneath 1:28

Think Gemini. A semester-long project, answering the Young Ones Google brief. Before they generated anything they ran empathy interviews with students and instructors, and built the whole thing from what they found.

Let me be clear about what this is. My students, my course, my pilot. Not independent validation. What I did not do is show them my rubric.

I asked Thalia to walk me through the judgment calls in her own words.

The teacher character. The team wanted a racially diverse cast, and text prompts would not produce one. Ten to twenty generations, and she was not getting there by writing better sentences.

That is the part worth naming. Prompting is not just words and description. When words stop working you have other moves: negative prompting, reference images, changing what you feed the model instead of what you say to it.

So she stopped rewriting the prompt and changed the structure instead. She added a second reference image, for likeness, on top of the team's art direction reference. Style from one, person from the other.

All four team members are credited in small type under the quote attribution, and again on the video slide. On the slide deliberately, not read aloud.

"Sometimes words aren't enough to tell the AI what we want. But it also made us think, maybe there is bias within the AI itself. Maybe it's not intentional, but it does happen."

She diagnosed a model limitation, separated it from her own prompting, and did not accuse anyone of intent. That is the Representational Error from the previous slide. I had already seen the pattern in my own work. Hearing a student describe it unprompted, in her own words, is what convinced me it needed a category of its own.

Delivery: read the quote as written, then stop for a full second. Don't explain it before the room has heard it.

Slide 7: What She Did Next 1:10

When the team defined how they would measure whether their idea worked, one of their four metrics was whether a student could explain the reasoning behind their decisions without looking back at what the AI had said. That is almost exactly my seventh CPA dimension, written by a student who had never seen my rubric.

So I asked her how she arrived at it. She told me she would throw it out. Too abstract, written in a time crunch while they were preparing the submission. Then she gave me four better ones, all traced back to the pain points from her own interviews.

I asked what made it obvious months later that was not obvious then. She had presented the project many times, people kept asking questions, and somewhere in answering them she realized there was a better way to think about it.

Nobody was grading her. She reopened her own decision, named why it was weak, and rebuilt it out of her research. That is reflective reasoning. Not a measure of it, the thing itself. And no rubric made her do it. It happened because she kept presenting the work in portfolio reviews and job interviews, and people kept asking her questions. Which is exactly why I want a rubric that asks for it on purpose, in every critique.

Slide: her original KPI on the left, greyed and struck through. Four revised KPIs on the right. Her sentence pulled out large: "I honestly think these are a bit too abstract and outdated."

Delivery: this is the turn. Pause before "she told me she'd throw it out," not after. The room expects me to defend the framework and I am doing the opposite. Don't soften her critique into "she offered some refinements" or the slide is lost.

Slide 9: The Risk I Worried About 0:33 · cut 2

Students can experience evidence capture as surveillance rather than support. That is the objection I was most worried about, so I asked Thalia directly. She had never seen my rubric.

"I wouldn't recommend 100 percent written, simply because it's easy for people to just AI it. Which sounds horrible, but trust, it will probably happen."

My rubric already verifies through live presentation, not a written log. She got there from the other side of the desk, for a reason I had never put as well: a written trail can be generated, a spoken one can't. Same decision, two directions. Full map on the Institute page.

Slide: two blocks. Documentation bias and student IP moved to the leave-behind on Aug 17. They were assertions of humility with no content behind them, and the IP one undercut the protectable-IP claim for no gain.

Slide 10: Open Source, and the Book 1:24

CPA doesn't depend on a licensing tier or on detection software, so it can work for any program facing this gap, not only the well resourced ones. So I'm giving it away.

  1. The Field Guide is open source. Take it. Use it in whatever you teach, not only design. Change what doesn't fit. You don't need my permission and you don't need to tell me
  2. The AI Pedagogy Toolkit is where this goes next. Open source, cross-discipline, so nobody has to start from a blank page

I'm sharing this as open source, for the design education community.

Empowering design education with AI, with my co-author C.J. Yeh. CPA is Chapter 2 and the Field Guide. Much of the rest belongs to other people: our students, and the industry professionals who talked honestly with us about where this is going. The field guide is the part we most want you to steal. It's in there so you don't have to build yours from scratch.

Slide: the two asks. Final slide is the book cover, both names, QR code. Nothing else, because I'll be holding the object.

Delivery: this slide asks for nothing, and that is the whole posture. Twenty years of work, a book, and a framework, handed to the field. Don't soften "you don't need my permission and you don't need to tell me" into an invitation to stay in touch. The generosity only reads as generosity if there is no hook in it. Aug 17: three asks became two, then none. Asking at all was the wrong frame, because this is contributed research, not a request for help.

Rules for this talk

  • Ten minutes still rewards one clear argument. The temptation is to fill the extra four minutes with what the six-minute version dropped. Don't. Spend all four on the case study
  • Credit all four Think Gemini team members by name, on the slide and out loud. This is a team project and the room will notice which way it is handled
  • Be precise about what is retrospective and what is prospective. Think Gemini was not scored under CPA, it predates the CT302 rollout. Say so. The awards prove the underlying skill is real and valued; the periodic-presentation verification layer is what CPA adds going forward
  • Written in the book's voice, not a keynote voice. Where the approach is contrasted with others, stay descriptive. The room should feel invited
